Decision theorist, formal epistemologist, and philosopher of science and of AI.

I am an Assistant Professor of Philosophy at UNC Chapel Hill, Core Faculty for the UNC PPE Program, and co-director of ∃xProof AI with Aydin Mohseni. I work on the foundations of AI safety and alignment, on the foundations of rational agency, and on the philosophy of science more broadly.

Inquiring together

I think of inquiry as something we do together. We start in different places, keep our minds open, and let the evidence carry us toward the truth. Research

AI safety and alignment

When does an AI system have beliefs and goals, and how could we measure them? When should we delegate a decision to an AI system whose values may not quite match ours? With Ben Levinstein I have written Radical AI Interpretability, a book on the conceptual foundations of interpretability, forthcoming with Cambridge University Press.

Take delegation. An AI system may know far more than we do and still want something slightly different. I develop models that help us understand when an agent’s capabilities pay for the cost of its misalignment.

Rational agency

I study the foundations of rational agency. For example, how should a rational agent act on the evidence she has? If you know what a better-informed version of yourself would do, should you already do it? I also take notions that look metaphysically loaded, such as chance, causation, or control, and try to naturalize them by showing that they can be recovered from an agent’s own beliefs and desires.

Take chance. The triangle at the top of the page cheats a little, since its hypotheses are chances. De Finetti showed how to do without them. Credences over chances are a way of representing exchangeable credences about observables, such as the outcomes of rolls of a die, not beliefs about something extra in the world. The figure shows this with the die from the top of the page; you can switch it to a coin.

Formal philosophy of science

I use formal models to ask what justifies the methods of the sciences, including machine learning, and how scientific and social practices can emerge.

Take language. In Lewis–Skyrms signaling games, meaning emerges from repeated interaction alone, with no prior agreement about what anything means. With Jacob VanDrunen I extend these games so that agents must also learn which features of their environment carry signals in the first place. Natural salience, it turns out, can hold the evolution of signaling back.
